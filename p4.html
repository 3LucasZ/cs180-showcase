In Project 4A we stitch together images taken from the same CoP but at different angles into a mosaic / panorama / wide-camera. During this process we: (1) estimate the projective transformation between shots, (2) inverse warp to a reference frame, and (3) blend the images together.

A.1: Shoot the Pictures

For this project, I took images of 3 different scenes: a rooftop portrait over downtown Berkeley, campus with the Campanile, and the new CDSS Gateway building interior. For each scene, I took 3 images: left, center, right with substantial overlap between each scene. I stabilized my iPhone on a horizontal surface and rotated it over the CoP's axis.

A.2: Recover homographies

For the process, we only need a function that is able to combine 2 images. Once we can combine 2 images, we can combine (1,2) then combine ((1,2),3).

First, we pick correspondences between the 2 images by hand. We use these to define the homography \(p' = Hp\): a \(3\times3\) matrix with eight DoF (bottom-right entry is constant 1). In homogeneous coordinates it reads

\[ \begin{bmatrix} w'x' \\ w'y' \\ w' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\ h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\ h_7 & h_8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

so multiplying it out gives \(w' = h_7x + h_8y + 1\). Dividing each image coordinate by that,

\[ \begin{aligned} x' &= \frac{h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3}{h_7 x + h_8 y + 1}, \\ y' &= \frac{h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6}{h_7 x + h_8 y + 1} \end{aligned} \]

and clearing the denominators and collecting the eight unknowns on the left turns each correspondence into two linear equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3 - h_7 x x' - h_8 y x' &= x', \\ h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6 - h_7 x y' - h_8 y y' &= y' \end{aligned} \]

Each correspondence adds two rows to \(Ah = b\),

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_1 \\ h_2 \\ h_3 \\ h_4 \\ h_5 \\ h_6 \\ h_7 \\ h_8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} \]

Stack all the rows over all \(n\) correspondences. We get \(A\) of size \(2n\times8\) and \(b\) of length \(2n\). This is an over-determined system and can be solved by least squares (thanks EECS127)!

\[ \hat{\mathbf h} = \arg\min_{\mathbf h} \lVert A\mathbf h - \mathbf b \rVert^2 = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \mathbf b \]

and \(H\) is the eight entries + with the constant 1 put back:

\[ H = \begin{bmatrix} h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\ h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\ h_7 & h_8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The correspondences were done manually by clicking the point in one photo and its 'bijection' in the other.

Above, the least-squares has an error of ~14.77 pixel units and finds the homography:

\[ H = \begin{bmatrix} 0.57 & 0.02 & 1376 \\ -0.17 & 0.86 & 194\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] pinroom Tool I made to place the correspondences! open pinroom

A.3: Warp the images

Now that we have the homography, we must run inverse warping on each output pixel, to know the corresponding input pixel, and apply its RGB value. For this, we create out output meshgrid, apply \(H^{-1}\) to the coordinages, and it yields the corresopnding input coordinates. I made 2 samples: nearest-neighbor and bilinear interpolation.

To test that our inverse warper works, we run rectification. Rectification is warping target points on a projectively transformed flat surface onto the corners of a rectangle, so the plane can be seen straight-on.

A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Ok, so now we have a way to compute the homography H, and apply it to the image we want to transform to create a new image. However, if we naiively just combine the images together (e.g averaging out the overlapping part) after doing this, we get an ugly result:

Our old enemy from project 2! Ugly seams! This is caused by many factors, e.g different exposures between photographs, wind blowing some objects into different locations so their not exactly in the same place. But we know exactly how to deal with this. Laplacian pyramid stack multi-resolution blending :D.

1: we extend the image color past the warp mask by using the color of the nearest colored pixel (so black fill doesn't get inside the blend).

2: We color normalize both left and right images to be the mean of the intersection (fight exposure difference).

3: utilize a two-band Laplacian pyramid, copying over code from project 2 (to blend seamlessly)

Yay! Seamless!
