In Project 4A we stitch together images taken from the same CoP but at different angles into a mosaic / panorama / wide-camera. During this process we: (1) estimate the projective transformation between shots, (2) inverse warp to a reference frame, and (3) blend the images together.

A.1: Shoot the Pictures

For this project, I took images of 3 different scenes: a rooftop portrait over downtown Berkeley, campus with the Campanile, and the new CDSS Gateway building interior. For each scene, I took 3 images: left, center, right with substantial overlap between each scene. I stabilized my iPhone on a horizontal surface and rotated it over the CoP's axis.

A.2: Recover homographies

For the process, we only need a function that is able to combine 2 images. Once we can combine 2 images, we can combine (1,2) then combine ((1,2),3).

First, we pick correspondences between the 2 images by hand. We use these to define the homography \(p' = Hp\): a \(3\times3\) matrix with eight DoF (bottom-right entry is constant 1). In homogeneous coordinates it reads

\[ \begin{bmatrix} w'x' \\ w'y' \\ w' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\ h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\ h_7 & h_8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

so multiplying it out gives \(w' = h_7x + h_8y + 1\). Dividing each image coordinate by that,

\[ \begin{aligned} x' &= \frac{h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3}{h_7 x + h_8 y + 1}, \\ y' &= \frac{h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6}{h_7 x + h_8 y + 1} \end{aligned} \]

and clearing the denominators and collecting the eight unknowns on the left turns each correspondence into two linear equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3 - h_7 x x' - h_8 y x' &= x', \\ h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6 - h_7 x y' - h_8 y y' &= y' \end{aligned} \]

Each correspondence adds two rows to \(Ah = b\),

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_1 \\ h_2 \\ h_3 \\ h_4 \\ h_5 \\ h_6 \\ h_7 \\ h_8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} \]

Stack all the rows over all \(n\) correspondences. We get \(A\) of size \(2n\times8\) and \(b\) of length \(2n\). This is an over-determined system and can be solved by least squares (thanks EECS127)!

\[ \hat{\mathbf h} = \arg\min_{\mathbf h} \lVert A\mathbf h - \mathbf b \rVert^2 = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \mathbf b \]

and \(H\) is the eight entries + with the constant 1 put back:

\[ H = \begin{bmatrix} h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\ h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\ h_7 & h_8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The correspondences were done manually by clicking the point in one photo and its 'bijection' in the other.

Above, the least-squares has an error of ~14.77 pixel units and finds the homography:

\[ H = \begin{bmatrix} 0.57 & 0.02 & 1376 \\ -0.17 & 0.86 & 194\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] pinroom Tool I made to place the correspondences! open pinroom

A.3: Warp the images

Now that we have the homography, we must run inverse warping on each output pixel, to know the corresponding input pixel, and apply its RGB value. For this, we create out output meshgrid, apply \(H^{-1}\) to the coordinages, and it yields the corresopnding input coordinates. I made 2 samples: nearest-neighbor and bilinear interpolation.

To test that our inverse warper works, we run rectification. Rectification is warping target points on a projectively transformed flat surface onto the corners of a rectangle, so the plane can be seen straight-on.

A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Ok, so now we have a way to compute the homography H, and apply it to the image we want to transform to create a new image. However, if we naiively just combine the images together (e.g averaging out the overlapping part) after doing this, we get an ugly result:

Our old enemy from project 2! Ugly seams! This is caused by many factors, e.g different exposures between photographs, wind blowing some objects into different locations so their not exactly in the same place. But we know exactly how to deal with this. Laplacian pyramid stack multi-resolution blending :D.

1: we extend the image color past the warp mask by using the color of the nearest colored pixel (so black fill doesn't get inside the blend).

2: We color normalize both left and right images to be the mean of the intersection (fight exposure difference).

3: utilize a two-band Laplacian pyramid, copying over code from project 2 (to blend seamlessly)

Yay! Seamless!

Part B: Automatic Mosaics

Part B: we no longer manually find correspondences. Now, we do automatic feature finding and alignment. The steps explored here are: Harris corners, adaptive non-maximal suppression (ANMS), MOPS feature descriptor, Lowe's ratio test, and RANSAC.

B.1: Harris Corner Detection and ANMS

Harris corner detection works by finding patches where if you move the patch in any direction, the patch changes by a lot.

Here's the measure of change in patch \(W\) for a shift \([u, v]\):

\[ \begin{aligned} E(u,v) &= \sum_{(x,y)\in W} \left[ I(x+u,\, y+v) - I(x,y) \right]^2 \\ &\approx \sum_{(x,y)\in W} \left[ I(x,y) + \begin{bmatrix} I_x & I_y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} - I(x,y) \right]^2 \\ &= \sum_{(x,y)\in W} \left( \begin{bmatrix} I_x & I_y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} \right)^2 \\ &= \sum_{(x,y)\in W} \begin{bmatrix} u & v \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

Thus, we find that

\[ E(u,v) \approx \begin{bmatrix} u & v \end{bmatrix} M \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} \]

where \(M\) is a "second moment" matrix, where elements are found by computing derivatives in the pixels:

\[ M = \sum_{(x,y)\in W} \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Thus, we get the final equation to find the "corner response" of a pixel in the image:

\[ \begin{aligned} R &= \frac{\det M}{\operatorname{Trace} M} \\ \det M &= \lambda_1\lambda_2 \\ \operatorname{Trace} M &= \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \end{aligned} \]

Pixels with a corner response over the threshold, is classified as a corner. Here are the harris corners detected:

This is much better, but all the points seem to be clustered around the same landmarks. It's more robust to have corner feature points detected more uniformly throughout the scene.

Thus, we try an algorithm: Adaptive Non-maximal Suppression (ANMS), instead of naiively picking the top K best corner responses. In ANMS, we calculate the minimum suppression radius \(r_i\) for each of our harris corners:

\[ r_i = \min_j \left| \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \right|, \quad \text{s.t. } f(\mathbf{x}_i) < c_{\mathrm{robust}} f(\mathbf{x}_j), \;\; \mathbf{x}_j \in \mathcal{I} \]

This simplifies down to:

\[ r_i = \min_{j:\, h_i < 0.9\, h_j} \left\| \boldsymbol{p}_i - \boldsymbol{p}_j \right\|_2 \]

The intuition is that we prefer corners that are far away from significantly stronger corners. Now, we pick the top 250 corners with the best \(r_i\) values.

B.2: Feature Descriptor Extraction

Now that we have the feature locations of images via corners, we have to turn them into actual features that can be used. A good feature is one that can be corresponded with unambiguously to the matching feature in the overlapping image.

We use the Multi-Scale Oriented Patches (MOPS) algorithm to turn a corner into a feature descriptor.

  1. capture the 40x40 pixel region around the corner as greyscale.
  2. blur (to avoid aliasing) and sample it down to 8x8
  3. normalize the patch so that it is 0 mean and 1 stddev (intensity-invariance)

Below, we take some of our corners and visualize their corresponding feature descriptors (color-coded)!

B.3: Feature Matching

Now, suppose we have extracted the feature descriptors from 2 images we want to stitch together. Now, the problem is corresponding them to one another; how can we match a feature in one image to a feature in another image. 2 feature descriptors corresponding to the same feature should be very similar in euclidean distance. Thus, we use Lowe's ratio test. For each feature in the first image, we find the most similar feature descriptor in the 2nd image as its match. The match is used only if its error is < 0.6x the second nearest neighbor.

We use the threshold of 0.6x since it does a great job of separating the correct from the incorrect points. From a Data 100 standpoint: not linearly separable, we can definitely do a more accurate ROC curve, type 1 / 2 analysis, etc.

B.4: 4-Point RANSAC

As you can tell, it's still not perfect. Many of the matches found are not true matches and errors from our algorithm. We run 1 final algorithm to separate the matches into "inliers" that are correct, and "outliers" that are incorrect.

We use the RANdom SAmple Consensus algorithm (RANSAC) for this.

  1. repeat many times:
  2. pick 4 random matches
  3. create the homography H which transforms from left-part of the matches to the right-part of the matches
  4. find how many matches are well-solved by the homography H (the left-part, when applied H, becomes very close to the right-part). These are called the inliers.
  5. If the number of inliers is more than previous trials, use that as the set of best inliers.

These final inliers are our final correspondences, replacing the manually hand-clicked points in Part A.

These correspondences look really accurate!

B.5: Bells and Whistles — Panorama

Me and some friends did a fun hike around Angel Island. I took some photos, albeit horrible ones. They weren't straight / rotated properly, subjects were moving, and many other things went wrong. However, I want to see if I can recover a panorama from it.

For the first 3 images, I use the same automatic stitching algorithm from before.

The resulting image is very wide / distorted. This is because we are projecting each frame onto one rectangular / perspective plane. Idea: wawarprping each frame onto a cylinder first.

For an output pixel measured from the cylinder's center, the source pixel is found by

\[ \theta = \frac{u}{f}, \qquad x = f\tan\theta + c_x, \qquad y = \frac{v}{\cos\theta} + c_y \]

where \(f\) is the focal length in pixels and \((c_x, c_y)\) the center of the source frame. The cylinder only covers the angle the camera sees, so the output is trimmed to

\[ W' = 2\left\lfloor f\arctan \frac{c_x}{f} \right\rfloor + 1 \]
