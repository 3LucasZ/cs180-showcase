Introduction

Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii travelled across Russia taking thousands of amazing photographs. Each scene, he recorded 3 images with different exposures on different glass plates: red, green, and blue filter. I am thankful to the genius of Prokudin-Gorskii for taking these glass plate images. Because of his imagination and efforts, we can realize what the Russian Empire looked like in the past.

However, if you simply try to place the plates on top of each other, it looks really off.

We need an algorithm to align them. This will take some experimentation. They seem to be rotated correctly, so we just need to shift each image \(x, y\) to align.

Single-scale alignment

Our first idea to align a grey-scale image with another grey-scale image is via brute force search. We search over every single possible (x,y) shift alignment and take the the best one. This will take very long if we try every single shift e.g 350x350. Luckily, it doesn't seem like we have to shift too much, as from the image above the error margin looks small. For a small image with a small shift, we find that it suffices to just check shifts in the x and y direction within \([-w, w]\) pixels, where we tried a half-width value of \(w = 32\).

Error metric

To know a certain alignment is the "best" one, we assign a score to each alignment trial. \(\text{Score} = \text{similarity\_function}(u, v_i)\) where we turn the reference image to vector \(u\) and image we shifted to vector \(v_i\). There are 2 simple possibilities for a score: euclidean distance and normalized cross correlation. Euclidean distance measures the difference of 2 vectors by: \( \mathrm{ed}(u,v)=\sqrt{\sum_i (u_i-v_i)^2} \). NCC measures the similarity of 2 vectors by: \( \mathrm{ncc}(u,v)=\frac{\sum_i (u_i-\bar{u})(v_i-\bar{v})} {\sqrt{\sum_i (u_i-\bar{u})^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i (v_i-\bar{v})^2}} \). This is the dot product (cosin similarity) between normalized vectors. After some testing, I found NCC to be much more robust due to the normalization.

We perform our alignment algorithm twice: align green to blue first, then align red to blue. Then, all 3 glass plates will be aligned as a single image. 2 of the 3 images align properly from this experiment.

Pyramids

Unfortunately, this did not work on the Cathedral, and will certainly not work on the much larger .tif images (~70MB each!). Shifts of e.g 32 pixels is no where near enough. We will need a more efficient and powerful algorithm to align them.

So here's a really cool recursive algorithm idea!

If we take the large images and downsample it really small to e.g \(100 \times 100\), our algorithm above would work quickly on a small \(w\). However, if applying that shift vector (multiplied by the upscale of 32) on the original size e.g \(3200 \times 3200\), we find that the alignment is very coarse. There are many possibilities e.g \(32 \times 32\) of a finer alignment (x/y +/- 16) after upscaling. However, the number of possibilities has drastically reduced.

The idea is to downsample the 2 images we want to align until really small. Then, we run the align algorithm for a small half-width \((w)\) of e.g 4 pixels. We then go to the next image which is downscaled less and run the align algorithm again. We do this recursively: taking a less downscaled image and adding a new micro-alignment vector with a small halfsize. Once we are back to our original sized image, its well aligned! Nearly all the images align properly at this point.

Downsampling

I used the \(\frac{1}{4}\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) standard box-filter downsampling kernel. It may have been better to downsample using a Gaussian filter, but both worked on the samples.

The Sobel Prize

The emir is quite stubborn and not lining up... We need a more clever approach to make it work.

One observation is that the color intensities are fundamentally way too different in between images, so even though we normalize them, they won't line up via NCC properly. Our idea is to make an outline of the emir instead of the raw image itself. This is doable via image partial derivatives and gradients. The kernel: \([-1, 1]\) can be used to find the horizontal edges. It's transpose can be used to find the vertical edges. Taking the magnitude, gives us the gradient:

Each of the 3 glass plates looks much more similar now, and it seems more feasible that they will successfully line up.

Note: We could probably do better: smooth first to remove noise before doing edge (partial derivative) detection. There are also other techniques: Canny edge detection, laplacian edge detection. But, the emir is lining up perfectly now!

Nature is Beautiful

This isn't good enough. Some of the images still "look" wrong. I want a beautiful National-Geographic type high-res nature image. Compare the very mediocre lake image we have with an actual professional photography image:

Step 1. Normalize each color channel disjointly: make R, G, B each take up the entire range. E.g if Red took up \([63, 128]\), we stretch the colors to take on the entire \([0, 255]\) intensity range. I found that it looked nicer than normalizing all channels together.

Step 2. Automatic white balance: we take the prior that the brightest objects should be white in the image. To be honest, I don't think this did anything.

Step 3. We sharpen the image, to try to bring out more detail.

Step 4. Finally, we increase the vibrance: increasing saturation / intensity of muted colors and not changing already high-intensity colors. Here is the final product. Although, still looks nothing like modern professional photography, it's better than the original.
