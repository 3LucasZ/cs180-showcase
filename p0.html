Becoming friends with your camera

Perspective, focal length, and the center of projection — a close-up portrait done the wrong way and the right way, architectural compression, and a dolly zoom.

The wrong way, the right way

Get close and the camera has to swing wide to fit the whole face, so the near features balloon — the nose, the brows, the hand that reaches toward the lens. Then step back, zoom in to keep the face the same size, and the relationship between the features settles into something the eyes already know.

The difference is the center of projection. Wide angle means the camera is inches from the face, so the rays from different parts of the face diverge sharply and near parts map to very different places. Telephoto pushes the camera back, the rays converge nearly parallel, and every part of the face is rendered at roughly the same scale. The face stops being a funhouse and starts being a face.

Architectural perspective compression

Photograph a building from close and the facade leans back toward the corners, windows stretching away. Walk far away, zoom in so the building fills the frame the same way, and the same facade flattens into a nearly orthographic plane — the depth between near and far columns collapses.

From close, the depth from the near corner to the far corner is a large fraction of the viewing distance, so the geometry reads as depth. From far away, that same depth is a tiny fraction of the distance, so the two corners land almost at the same place on the sensor. The compression is real foreshortening, and the whole scene collapses toward a single point — a reminder that perspective is a property of where you stand, not of the lens.

A second example, same street, far to close.

The dolly zoom

Move the camera back while zooming in to keep the subject framed: the subject holds its size while the world behind it stretches and rushes. Ten frames of an android, from close to far.

As the focal length climbs, the field of view narrows; moving back returns the subject to the same place on the sensor. The background can't do the same arithmetic, so it either swells to fill the narrowing frame or gets pushed past the edges — the whole world breathing around a subject that refuses to move.
