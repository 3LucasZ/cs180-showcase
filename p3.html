CS 180 · project 03

Fun with Flow Models

lucas zheng · github.com/3LucasZ/cs180-showcase

Flow models learn a velocity field that carries noise to data along a straight line. At time \(t\) the training input is \(x_t=(1-t)\epsilon+t x_1\): pure noise at \(t=0\), a clean image at \(t=1\). Training regresses the velocity \(x_1-\epsilon\); sampling starts from noise and integrates the prediction forward. Both halves of this project use that one idea.

Part A: Exploring the Power of Flow Models

Part A never trains anything. It samples a pretrained flow model, PixNerd-XXL-P16-T2I, and uses its velocity predictions to denoise, generate, edit, and combine images. Unless a section says otherwise, sampling is Euler integration with step size \(h=0.02\), classifier-free guidance \(w=7\), and seed 180. The image throughout sections 1.1 to 1.5 is my own: a photo at Niagara Falls, center-cropped to \(512\times512\) and rescaled to \([-1,1]\).

1.1: Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

\(\epsilon\) is one fixed Gaussian tensor, reused at every time. Mixing it with the clean image gives the three inputs below; the endpoints are worth checking, since \(t=0\) must return the noise tensor exactly and \(t=1\) the original image. At \(t=0.25\) the photo is only visible as a faint silhouette; by \(t=0.75\) it is mostly the photo, with a film of noise over it.

1.2: Classical Denoising

Classical denoising has no model. Dividing the noisy image by \(t\) restores the original amplitude, and a Gaussian blur removes what is left. The blur strength is chosen by sweeping \(\sigma\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}\) and keeping the result with the lowest MSE against the clean photo. The winning \(\sigma\) is largest where the noise dominates: \(8\) at \(t=0.25\), \(4\) at \(0.50\), \(2\) at \(0.75\). A wider blur averages away more noise, and more of the image with it, so the baseline buys its smoothness by giving up detail.

1.3: One-Step Denoising

One prediction from the network replaces the whole sweep. The model is asked for the velocity at the noisy input with the prompt “a high quality photo”, and that velocity is added once: \(\hat x_1=x_t+(1-t)v_\theta(x_t,t,c)\). Even at \(t=0.25\), where the input looks like static, the estimate recovers the falls, the railing, and the person. What it does not recover is detail: the image is soft, as if the model has averaged over the ways the scene could have looked.

1.4: Visualizing Velocity

The velocity the model predicts is one step toward the clean image, so it can be inspected directly. Two updates are compared: the model's \((1-t)\hat v_t\), and the update that would recover my original exactly, \((1-t)v_{gt}=x_1-x_t\). Both are drawn on the same symmetric color scale with gray at zero, so brightness is magnitude and hue is sign. The error map is RMS across RGB at every pixel, on one linear scale shared by all three times. Predicted and true agree on the large shapes and disagree most on edges — the railing, the face, the lip of the falls — where the clean image is least determined by the noise.

1.5: Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

One step is a large jump. Euler sampling breaks it into a march: \(x_{t+h}=x_t+h\,v_\theta(x_t,t)\), with the velocity recomputed at every state and the last step shortened so the path lands exactly on \(t=1\). Starting from the same \(t=0.50\) input, 25 steps of \(h=0.02\) are shown below: the input, every fifth update, and the result. The final image keeps the detail the one-step estimate blurred away. Set against section 1.2, it is the difference between a model that knows what a photograph looks like and a Gaussian that only knows how to average.

1.6: Generating Images from Noise

Five independent noise tensors, seeds 180 through 220, integrated from \(t=0\) to \(1\) in 50 Euler steps. The prompt is the same generic “a high quality photo” used above. The seeds give five different compositions, but the prompt narrows what kind of photograph they are: the variation is in the scene, not in the style. That is worth recording, because it is the model's default idea of a photograph, before any guidance or editing.

2.1: Classifier-Free Guidance

Classifier-free guidance makes the prompt matter more. The velocity is evaluated twice on the same state and time — once with the prompt, once with the empty string — and the unconditional prediction is subtracted from the conditional one: \(v_{cfg}=v_c+w(v_c-v_u)\). At \(w=0\) this is ordinary conditional sampling; at \(w=7\) the conditional direction is amplified sevenfold. Using the same five initial noises as section 1.6 isolates the effect: with the same seeds, alignment to the prompt goes up and the five images grow more alike. Guidance trades diversity for adherence, and the trade continues as \(w\) rises.

3.1: Image-to-Image Editing

An edit is a generation that starts partway down the path. The clean image is re-noised to a chosen time, \(x_{t_s}=(1-t_s)\epsilon+t_s x_1\), and the sampler runs from \(t_s\) to \(1\). A small \(t_s\) starts near noise and gives the model room to rebuild the image; a large \(t_s\) starts close to the original and preserves it. Seven starts are used throughout: \(0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.10, 0.14, 0.25, 0.50\). At \(0.50\) the result is barely distinguishable from the input. The first real changes appear around \(0.25\); below \(0.10\), the scene is rebuilt and only the composition survives.

The same seven times are then applied to one image from the web and to my own drawings. Line art is the interesting case: with little color and little texture, the prompt has to invent most of the result, and the drawing's structure is what keeps it anchored.

3.2: Text-Guided Image Editing

The prompt now describes the edit rather than the photograph. The Campanile becomes “Gandalf uses his wand to get the infinity stones”; my photo becomes “Student studying computer science discrete math at UC Berkeley”; my second image becomes “Beautiful island can be seen through the window of an airplane”. The start time decides how much of the prompt can take hold. At \(t_s=0.50\) the output is the original with a faint suggestion of the prompt; at \(0.02\) the prompt has nearly full control and the original contributes composition only.

3.3: Visual Anagrams

A visual anagram is one image that reads as two subjects. The sampler runs the conditional velocity for prompt \(a\) on the current state, and the conditional velocity for prompt \(b\) on the state rotated \(180^\circ\); the second result is rotated back and the two are averaged: \(v=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(v_{cfg}(x,t,c_a)+F\,v_{cfg}(F x,t,c_b)\big)\). Each prompt only ever sees its own orientation, so the model agrees to draw both at once. Upright, the image follows \(a\); turned around, it follows \(b\).

3.4: Hybrid Images

Hybrid images use the same frequency split as project 2, but inside the sampler. At every step the low frequencies of prompt \(a\)'s velocity are combined with prompt \(b\)'s velocity minus its own low frequencies: \(v=LP(v_a)+v_b-LP(v_b)\), with a Gaussian low-pass of \(\sigma=16\) and kernel \(265\). Prompt \(a\) determines the distant structure; prompt \(b\) determines the close-up detail. Each result is shown at full size and reduced to a small thumbnail, which is the viewing distance the trick depends on.

Part B: Flow Matching from Scratch

Part B builds the same machinery from scratch. The data is MNIST, the model is a small UNet with hidden width \(D=64\), and the target is again the velocity \(x_1-x_0\). The sequence is deliberate: first a plain denoiser with no time input, then a time-conditioned flow model, then a class-conditioned one with guidance.

1.2: Using the UNet to Train a Denoiser

The denoiser is trained on pairs: a digit plus Gaussian noise at a fixed level \(\sigma=0.5\), and the clean digit as the target. With no time input, the network learns one noise level rather than a family of them. Training uses batch 256, Adam at \(10^{-4}\), and five epochs.

1.2.1: Training

After one epoch the digits are clean but soft; the strokes firm up by the fifth. The samples below are the clean digit, the noisy input, and the model's output at each checkpoint.

1.2.2: Out-of-Distribution Testing

The model only ever saw \(\sigma=0.5\). Running it across the whole sweep shows where that assumption breaks. Near \(0.5\) it removes almost everything; at \(0.0\) it returns the digit nearly unchanged; at \(0.8\) and \(1.0\) it still finds the shape but leaves a grain of noise behind. A single noise level does not generalize into a denoiser for all levels.

1.2.3: Denoising Pure Noise

Training the same network with \(\sigma=1.0\) changes the problem. Every input is pure noise and the target is a random training digit, so the loss cannot tell which digit the noise was paired with. Under MSE, the best possible output is the average of all digits consistent with that noise — in practice, a blurry mean digit that looks like an 8. Both checkpoints converge to the same ghost, and more training does not sharpen it: the blur is the optimum, not a failure to converge.

2.1: Implementing a Time-conditioned UNet

For flow matching the network also receives the time. The UNet is a small convolutional encoder–decoder with skip connections; the normalized scalar \(t\) is projected by two FC blocks and added to the features at two depths. Training draws a time per image, forms \(x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1\), and regresses \(v=x_1-x_0\) with MSE. Nothing else changes from the denoiser.

2.2: Training the Time-conditioned UNet

Ten epochs, batch 64, Adam at \(10^{-2}\), with an exponential schedule that decays the rate to a tenth by the last epoch. The loss falls from the first optimizer step and keeps creeping down as the schedule softens.

2.3: Sampling from the Time-conditioned UNet

Sampling integrates from \(t=0\) to \(1\) in 50 Euler steps, holding the seed fixed across checkpoints so the rows differ only by training time. Epoch 1 already produces digits, but they are wobbly and grainy; by epoch 5 the strokes are stable; epoch 10 is clean. The model is unconditional, so each noise tensor becomes whichever digit the velocity field carries it to.

2.4: Implementing a Class-conditioned UNet

The class condition is a one-hot vector passed through its own pair of FC blocks, added alongside the time embedding. During training the class is dropped with probability \(0.1\), which teaches the same network an unconditional branch. That branch is what makes guidance possible at sampling time: the model can be asked for the class-conditional velocity and the unconditional velocity at the same state.

2.5: Training the Class-conditioned UNet

Training uses the same schedule as the time-conditioned model: ten epochs, batch 64, initial rate \(10^{-2}\), exponential decay to a tenth. Four samples per digit are drawn at each checkpoint.

2.6: Sampling from the Class-conditioned UNet

Every column is a requested digit and every row a different sample, four per class, drawn with 300 Euler steps and guidance \(g=5\). The noise is fixed across checkpoints. Epoch 1 already respects the class, with some malformed hybrids; epoch 5 cleans the strokes; epoch 10 gives four distinct, legible instances of each digit.

The last run removes the exponential schedule and holds a constant \(3\times10^{-3}\). One rate has to serve the whole run, so it is chosen between the scheduled run's initial \(10^{-2}\) and its final \(10^{-3}\): a compromise between fast early progress and fine late steps. The loss settles a little higher, and the epoch-10 digits keep slightly more variation in stroke weight, but the class is held just as well.
