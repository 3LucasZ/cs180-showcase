Maxwell's color matcher

The eye reads every color as three numbers — the firing of its red-, green-, and blue-sensitive cones. So any three fixed lights, mixed in the right amounts, can stand in for a color that isn't physically there. Screens, ink, and color photography all run on that trick.

Maxwell asked whether the trick always works. Below, a test light shines on the left half of a split field; your three lamps light the right. Match the halves. The lamps are imperfect on purpose — the green one is really a yellow-green — because real primaries always are.

The failure mode

No three real lamps can reproduce every color. Sweep the test light from the deep blues through the greens — about 470 to 555 nm — and the halves will never agree while all your lamps stay positive. The exact match calls for a negative red: less than none, which on the bench means moving the red lamp to light the test half instead of your own. Maxwell wrote exactly this down. It's the reason the 1931 CIE color-matching curves dip below zero around the greens, and why the CIE later invented imaginary primaries that never need to.
